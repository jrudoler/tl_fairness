# Literature review for Targeted Learning for Inference on Data Fairness

**Collection:** Zotero TL-fairness (`Q3LMFYZM`). **Review date:** October 8, 2026. **Manuscript:** the sections included by `paper/main.tex`, together with its estimand, assumption, double-robustness, threshold-stability, and CMI appendices. Updated after pulling Overleaf changes through manuscript commit d6876db. This review proposes changes; it does not edit the manuscript.

## Positioning the contribution

The manuscript has a useful distinction and a plausible methodological contribution: it estimates fairness disparities of the **population regression determined by a specified feature set**, with influence-function corrections for estimating that regression. The strongest case is the probabilistic demographic-parity and equal-opportunity estimands. It should not claim to introduce the general idea of data-generating-process fairness, population fairness inference, or the use of debiased machine learning in fairness.

Three references should anchor the positioning:

- **Cherian and Candès (2024)** provide statistical inference for auditing a fixed prediction rule, including simultaneous inference across groups. They answer the deployment question: how does this rule perform across populations? Your question is different: what disparity belongs to the population regression that the rule attempts to estimate? [Statistical Inference for Fairness Auditing](https://jmlr.org/papers/v25/23-0739.html).
- **Tang, Zhang, and Zhang (2023)** explicitly distinguish fairness with respect to the data-generating process, predicted outcomes, and induced impacts. This is direct conceptual prior work for separating data fairness from prediction fairness. Your contribution can be a precise statistical formulation and estimators, rather than first introducing the distinction. See Sections 6.2.1 and 6.3.1. [What-is and How-to for Fairness in Machine Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.04101).
- **Liu and Molinari (2025 version)** develop debiased machine-learning inference for a population fairness–accuracy frontier. This is the closest methodological challenge to a broad novelty claim. Their target is the feasible allocation of group risks and its frontier over a class of rules; yours is disparity of a particular population-defined, group-blind regression. [Inference for an algorithmic fairness-accuracy frontier](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08879).

A defensible positioning statement is:

> We study fairness of the population regression associated with a specified feature set. We define probabilistic demographic-parity and equal-opportunity disparities as functionals of this regression and derive influence-function estimators that correct for its estimation. This complements audits of fitted prediction rules and inference on attainable fairness–accuracy frontiers. We also examine conditional dependence and thresholded decisions, whose inferential requirements differ from those of the smooth score-based disparities.

This is a collection-based assessment of distinctiveness, not proof of priority throughout the field. The collection contains your group's [2025 preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04309), which is an earlier version of the same project and should not be counted as independent related work.

## The targets that related work must distinguish

Write $D_P(x)=P(Y=1\mid X=x)$, and let $m_T$ be a prediction rule fitted on training sample $T$. Group membership $G$ is available for analysis but is excluded from the inputs to $D_P$.

| Target | Example demographic-parity quantity | What the result describes |
|---|---|---|
| A fitted rule | $\theta_T(P)=E_P[m_T(X)\mid G=1]-E_P[m_T(X)\mid G=0]$ | Disparity of this trained rule, usually conditional on its training data |
| Your population regression | $\Psi(P)=E_P[D_P(X)\mid G=1]-E_P[D_P(X)\mid G=0]$ | Disparity of the population regression for the chosen inputs |
| A learning algorithm | $E_T[\theta_T(P)]$, or the distribution of $\theta_T(P)$ over retraining | Typical behavior or variability of a specified learning procedure |
| Attainable fairness and accuracy | Group risks over a class of possible decision rules | Feasible trade-offs and optimal rules under the specified information set |
| A causal intervention | A contrast involving potential outcomes or interventions on a graph | Effects of changing an attribute, decision, or causal pathway under identification assumptions |

Both the first and second rows are functionals of $P$, with the fitted rule held fixed in the first. Therefore, the current definition, “a functional of the observed data-generating distribution alone,” needs to specify **how the rule is determined by the distribution**. It otherwise does not clearly separate the manuscript from ordinary model auditing.

For your probabilistic parity target, the influence function for the group-specific mean contains

\[
\phi_g(O)=\frac{\pi_g(X)\{Y-D_P(X)\}+1\{G=g\}\{D_P(X)-\Psi_g(P)\}}{P(G=g)},
\qquad \pi_g(x)=P(G=g\mid X=x).
\]

The residual term is the concrete difference from averaging a fixed rule's predictions. For probabilistic equal opportunity, the corresponding correction uses $P(Y=1,G=g\mid X)$ and the positive-label group probability. This is a useful technical explanation of the contribution.

The framework is associative. It does not identify which historical mechanism caused disparity, correct a contaminated outcome label, estimate the average fairness of a retrained algorithm, or establish fairness of every model that could be trained on the data. It can describe the disparity of a group-blind population regression even when its predictors are proxies for group membership.

The proposed metrics also answer different questions. Conditional independence $Y\perp G\mid X$ does not imply zero score parity: groups can have different distributions of outcome-predictive $X$ even when CMI is zero. Your shared-predictor retraining design illustrates this under its nonzero alternatives. Conversely, equal group means can mask conditional dependence. Thus, even a valid CMI result would not certify all of the manuscript's data-fairness criteria; the Adult and LSAC interpretations should keep those criteria separate.

## Cherian and Candès in detail

**Correct author spelling:** John J. Cherian and Emmanuel J. Candès. The published paper is in JMLR, volume 25, 2024, pages 1–49. It is the most important fixed-rule auditing reference to add.

Their central disparity compares a group's expected loss or performance statistic with a reference value, using an independently trained rule. They bootstrap a process indexed by groups to obtain simultaneous bounds and certificates. Estimated reference values are allowed under asymptotic linearity and bootstrap consistency; their analysis already uses influence functions. Their framework also considers certain distribution shifts. [Published paper and PDF](https://jmlr.org/papers/v25/23-0739.html).

| Dimension | Cherian–Candès | Your manuscript | Positioning implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rule being evaluated | An existing fitted rule | $D_P$, or its thresholded decision | State the rule and target before discussing uncertainty |
| Learning error | The fitted rule is held fixed for the audit | Regression estimation enters the population target's expansion | Your residual correction changes the estimating equation |
| Inferential scope | Simultaneous bounds and certification over rich group classes | Mainly individual binary-group contrasts | Their multiplicity guarantees are an advantage your current results do not supply |
| Metric scope | Broad losses and performance measures | Specific parity, opportunity, and CMI functionals | Your narrower scope supports tailored estimand derivations |
| Statistical machinery | Bootstrap and empirical-process arguments, including an influence function for estimated reference values | One-step influence-function estimation and nuisance-error remainders | Avoid presenting influence functions as absent from previous auditing work |
| Thresholded rules | Audit the already fitted classifier | Estimate a classifier defined by thresholding an unknown regression | Your boundary-stability problem is additional |
| Decision supported | Assess a rule that may be deployed | Assess the population regression for a feature set | The methods are complementary |

**The distinction is an estimand mismatch, not evidence that their intervals are invalid.** Conditional on training, a fitted rule may truly have a disparity when $\Psi(P)=0$. A significant audit can correctly detect that disparity. A confidence interval for $\theta_T(P)$ need not cover $\Psi(P)$; that fact alone does not show failure of inference for $\theta_T(P)$.

This matters for your retraining experiment. Its observed pattern—model intervals retaining coverage of their own target while under-covering the population-regression target—is persuasive evidence that the questions differ. Describe a rejection as erroneous **for the population-regression null** when that is the comparison. Calling it a false positive without identifying the null suggests that the audit wrongly detected disparity of the deployed rule.

Two qualifications are necessary when summarizing their guarantees. Certification is asymptotic and depends on group-class complexity and nondegeneracy assumptions. Their proved FDR result for flagging is narrower than their general auditing framework: it concerns a finite group collection and a known reference, with additional conditions such as disjoint groups or binary loss; a general two-sided FDR theorem is not supplied. See Theorems 4 and 7, Proposition 8, and Appendices B–D. This is not a general finite-sample guarantee over arbitrary adaptive analyses. [Cherian–Candès](https://jmlr.org/papers/v25/23-0739.html).

For a fair empirical comparison, report coverage of both $\theta_T(P)$ and $\Psi(P)$, and explain which decision each interval supports. For your binary-group score contrast, a conventional fixed-rule interval is already sufficient to expose the target difference. Implementing their full simultaneous bootstrap is most informative if the experiment includes many overlapping groups; otherwise it adds a multiplicity correction to one method without a matching capability in yours.

Their equivalence-style certification also provides a useful interpretive lesson: failing to reject zero disparity does not establish that disparity is acceptably small. If the paper wants to **certify approximate fairness**, specify a substantive tolerance and evaluate whether an appropriate confidence set lies within it. A claim about simultaneous certification would require additional theory beyond your current pointwise intervals.

## Closest methodological and conceptual comparisons

### Population inference and robust auditing

**Huang–Hooker (2026), Fairness Testing for Algorithmic Pricing.** The new introduction cites this paper using huang2026fairness; the locally expanded bibliography also contains the same work under huangFairnessTestingAlgorithmic2026. Both keys are preserved after synchronization. The May 12, 2026 preprint explicitly assumes a fixed, known pricing function in Section 3.1. It studies regression projections of that function over randomly sampled audit profiles, sandwich covariance for those projections, paired coefficient comparisons, and tolerance-based decisions. [Full preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11614).

This is relevant prior work for distinguishing an audit's response, estimand, and source of uncertainty. However, it does **not** establish that regulators mistake model fairness for the manuscript's population-regression fairness. Its population projection target still concerns a fixed pricing algorithm. Your target instead depends on the unknown $D_P$. Two different corrections should remain separate: robust covariance for a possibly misspecified regression audit of a fixed function, and your residual correction for estimating the regression that defines a population fairness functional.

The introduction's new claim that increased uncertainty fails to detect bias also needs qualification. A conventional variance estimate can be too large or too small. Their paired-audit example concerns an omitted covariance between estimates from shared observations; their single-regression example concerns the variance of a projection under random profiles. Neither is a general demonstration that accounting for training uncertainty impairs fairness detection.

Suggested replacement:

> Huang and Hooker examine regression-based fairness audits of fixed deterministic pricing algorithms, showing that the covariance calculation must reflect the audit's sampling design and paired comparisons. Their analysis reinforces the need to distinguish the target and source of uncertainty: a population projection of a fixed pricing function differs from a fairness functional of an unknown population regression. We address the latter problem by correcting for estimation of the regression that defines the fairness target.

**Technical qualification before relying on its proxy-discrimination formula.** Proposition 3.1's projection influence function is $\Sigma^{-1}x\{f(z)-x^\top\beta^*\}$. Accordingly, two projections fitted on shared random profiles have joint influence functions involving their respective residuals. Their difference's asymptotic variance is the variance of the difference of these influence functions, divided by sample size. Proposition 3.3 and Appendix A.3 instead use raw covariate–response products and treat random projection weights as fixed in the repeated-sampling variance calculation. As written, that step requires further justification: an exactly linear deterministic response has zero estimation variance when fitted with its exact covariates, despite nonzero variance of raw covariate–response products. Thus, cite this preprint for its audit setup and motivation with care; its paired-regression formula should not be treated as a verified benchmark without resolving this issue. This is a mathematical assessment of the displayed formulas, not a reproduction of their empirical analysis.

**Liang, Lu, Mu, and Okumura (2024), Algorithm Design: A Fairness-Accuracy Frontier.** This is relevant prior work for the manuscript’s feature-choice motivation, beyond a generic citation about the cost of enforcing fairness. They define attainable pairs of group-specific expected losses over decision rules using a given information set, and order them by both groups’ accuracy and the disparity between their losses. Their input-design analysis considers a policymaker who controls available information and an agent who chooses a rule; their preferences can differ. Consequently, the effect of banning or supplying features depends on the information structure and these objectives. Their conditional results do not justify a universal statement that more features improve fairness or that feature removal does. Your analysis instead fixes the population regression associated with a feature set and estimates its disparity, rather than characterizing a frontier over rules or optimizing information supplied to another decision maker. The paper supplies theoretical context for feature choice; Liu–Molinari supply the closer inferential comparison. This assessment uses the setup, frontier, and input-design sections, rather than an end-to-end review of its 83-page proof appendices. [Preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09975).

**Liu–Molinari, fairness–accuracy frontier.** This paper deserves a full paragraph, not a survey citation. Its feasible set consists of the pair of group-specific expected losses induced by possible group-blind rules based on $X$. It estimates the support function using orthogonal scores and cross-fitting, then develops inference for feasible sets and frontiers. Section 4 and Theorem 4.1 make the debiasing connection explicit; Section 5 develops the inferential consequences. The results impose structural regularity on the nuisance functions and decision boundaries. They do not establish unrestricted root-$n$ inference for every optimal-rule functional. [Working paper](https://cemmap.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/CWP1325-Inference-for-an-algorithmic-accuracy-fairness-frontier.pdf).

Your contrast is between **estimating the unconstrained population regression's score disparity** and **estimating the feasible fairness–accuracy trade-off across rules**. Both depend on $P$ and the information available in $X$, and both use nuisance learning and first-order corrections. Neither “population target” nor “DML for fairness” can carry the novelty claim by itself. Their frontier is not automatically your estimand; different losses, score versus action outcomes, and rule classes must be matched before claiming one subsumes the other.

**Gao–Gronsbell, Infairness.** This is a second essential inference reference. The full paper explicitly conditions its targets and procedures on the classifier's training data. It combines a small labeled audit sample with a large unlabeled sample, using calibrated outcome imputation to estimate several fairness metrics. It establishes robustness to misspecification of its working imputation model and efficiency gains under additional conditions. Its assumptions include the sampling relationship between labeled and unlabeled data. [2026 manuscript version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12181), [Biometrics publication](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomtc/ujag142).

Your distinction is the population-regression target, rather than label-efficient auditing of a fixed classifier. You should acknowledge that statistically valid, robust inference assisted by learned nuisance models already exists for fairness auditing. Do not imply that all prior audit methods merely average predictions or universally ignore nuisance estimation. The local collection records the 2025 preprint; the retrieved version is from May 2026.

**FairTest, Tramèr et al.** FairTest is an auditing system for discovering unwarranted associations, not principally a method for preprocessing training data. It separates discovery and testing, reports statistical evidence, accounts for multiple comparisons, and allows explanatory factors. Its dependence measures include mutual information and conditional variants. Sections 3–4 are especially relevant. [Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.02377).

For your CMI discussion, its important overlap is conditional association as a diagnostic, and the dependence of the interpretation on which factors are acceptable explanations. Your joint conditional-law estimation and influence-function analysis differ from FairTest's association-testing implementation. However, using conditional dependence in a fairness audit is established prior work. Its multiplicity handling also highlights the limits of using your pointwise CMI output to select features adaptively.

### Data fairness and changes to data distributions

**Tang et al.** Their taxonomy is more directly relevant to your first contribution than generic preprocessing surveys. Sections 6.2.1 and 6.3.1 distinguish data/population fairness from prediction fairness and explain that fair data do not ensure fair predictions. Their framework is broader and heavily engages causal fairness; it does not supply your probabilistic parity estimators. Cite it to establish the existing distinction, then specify what you formalize statistically. [Survey and perspective](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.04101).

**Kamiran–Calders (2012)** explicitly start from discriminatory input data and develop suppression, relabeling, reweighing, and resampling for learning classifiers with reduced discrimination. **Calmon et al. (2017)** optimize a transformation of the joint data distribution under discrimination, distortion, and utility constraints, including generalization analysis. These papers make “working at the data level” an inadequate novelty claim. Your contribution estimates a property of an unmodified population law; these approaches construct altered training distributions to improve predictions. [Kamiran–Calders](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10115-011-0463-8), [Calmon et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03354).

**Leininger–Rittel–Bothmann (2025)** use a fictitious, normatively desired world in which protected attributes lack prohibited causal effects on the target. They connect causal preprocessing to fairness trade-offs. Your $P$ is the observed law, rather than a normatively chosen counterfactual law. This separates inference about recorded relationships from a prescription for repairing them. [Causal preprocessing paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14710).

**Kallus–Zhou (2018)** are particularly important for the phrase “underlying population.” Their selective-observation setting shows how fairness on a censored data distribution can differ from fairness in the deployment population. They study the distribution of Bayes scores and use sampling adjustments when suitable information is available. Your iid observed-law analysis does not by itself recover fairness for people whose outcomes are systematically unobserved. Also, their argument is more specific than the introduction's shorthand “if the outcome is biased”: the central mechanism is selection/censoring and population mismatch. [Residual Unfairness](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02887).

**Rezaei et al. (2021)** seek robust fair predictions under covariate shift with an invariant conditional label distribution. **Kim et al. (2022)** connect multicalibration to accurate outcome-mean estimation across target populations under a corresponding invariance assumption. These are relevant to transportability and the value of residual calibration, but they do not establish your particular parity contrast's influence function. State the iid population scope; do not describe the method as automatically handling distribution shifts. [Robust Fairness under Covariate Shift](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.05166), [Universal adaptability](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2108097119).

### Causality, feature choice, and policy outcomes

**Khademi et al. (2019)** define causal fairness contrasts using potential-outcome effects; **Zhang–Bareinboim (2018)** distinguish causal pathways in discriminatory decisions; **Kilbertus et al. (2017)** formulate constraints through causal reasoning. Your group contrast of $E[Y\mid X]$ is not an ATE of $G$: an ATE-style adjustment conditions the outcome regression on $G$ and averages over a specified covariate law. Your method avoids causal identification assumptions by answering an associative question. That is a scope choice, not a claim that causal explanations are unnecessary for all fairness decisions. [Khademi et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/3308558.3313559), [Zhang–Bareinboim](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v32i1.11564), [Kilbertus et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02744).

**Rehill (2023)** is a tempting comparison because of “double machine learning” in the title. It residualizes sensitive-variable effects and constructs counterfactually fair regression predictions under additive-separability assumptions. It adapts the DML framework to prediction rather than deriving inference for your population disparities. Acknowledge this use of causal machine learning in fairness, and then distinguish the target and inferential task. [Counterfactually fair regression with double machine learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11529).

**Ling et al. (2024)** select features using causal and conditional-independence reasoning. **Grgić-Hlača et al. (2016)** study process fairness as the perceived legitimacy of using particular features. These are distinct from both score parity and $I(Y;G\mid X)$. Your feature-set example adds an estimation perspective; it does not establish the first relationship between feature selection and fairness or tell the reader that conditioning variables are morally admissible. [Ling et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/3643890), [Process fairness paper](https://www.mlandthelaw.org/papers/grgic.pdf).

**Zuo et al. (2024)** accommodate partially known causal graphs, while **Cohausz et al. (2025)** address graph construction using data and background knowledge. Cite these if motivating practical limitations of causal fairness. Avoid saying every causal approach requires a fully specified true graph. Identification assumptions remain, even when graph uncertainty is represented. [Interventional fairness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10632), [Causal graphs and practical challenges](https://doi.org/10.1613/jair.1.19082).

**Bian et al. (2026)** distinguish fairness of actions from fairness of the consequences induced by those actions in policy learning. Their policy value and fairness objectives concern intervention-dependent outcomes. Your equal-opportunity functional conditions on observed positive labels; it is not fairness of the downstream outcome produced by a policy. The paper supports the discussion’s explicit statement that population fairness complements model fairness; the earlier opening’s stronger framing should remain consistent with that qualification. [Double Fairness Policy Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19186).

### Dependence measures, optimal rules, and applications

**Mesner–Shalizi** provide consistent nearest-neighbor CMI estimation for mixed discrete and continuous variables. Your estimator uses the binary joint conditional law and its margins. The contribution is this estimation strategy and its inferential analysis, not defining CMI or discovering that zero CMI characterizes conditional independence. Their consistency result is not itself a regular Wald test of independence. [CMI estimation](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.03387).

**Baharlouei et al., Rényi Fair Inference (ICLR 2020)** impose fairness through a dependence penalty during learning. “Inference” there primarily refers to constructing predictions or clusters. Its optimization guarantees should not be equated with confidence intervals for a population fairness parameter. It is relevant precedent for dependence-based fairness criteria. [Rényi Fair Inference](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.12005).

**Denis et al. (2024)** and **Xian–Yin–Zhao (2023)** characterize optimal classifiers subject to demographic parity and analyze estimated fair rules. Their use of population conditional probabilities and statistical guarantees weakens broad claims that earlier fairness theory only concerns one fitted model or has no account of estimation error. They target fairness-constrained decisions, rather than your unconstrained regression's disparity. Their multiclass/group settings also exceed your current binary scope. [Denis et al.](https://jmlr.org/papers/v25/23-0322.html), [Xian et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.01528).

**Liu–Vicente** optimize accuracy–fairness trade-offs; **Rodolfa–Lamba–Ghani** study their empirical size in public-policy applications. Neither estimates your functional, and neither should be used to assert that the trade-off is always large or always negligible. **FairSelect (2026)** evaluates combinations of mitigation methods and intersectional performance; **Fairlearn** supplies assessment and mitigation tools. These are useful practical context rather than the central theoretical comparison. [Liu–Vicente](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.01132), [Rodolfa et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.02972), [FairSelect](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08953), [Fairlearn](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16626).

**Lalor et al., FAIR-Frame (2024)** relate multivariate upstream representational errors to downstream allocation harms. This is a different use of “model-based” fairness, and supports separating pipeline harms from a single numerical disparity. It does not supply the same population-regression inference problem. This comparison uses the publisher's accessible article text; its PDF was unavailable. [FAIR-Frame](https://doi.org/10.1145/3641276).

**Ding et al. (2021)** challenge Adult's external validity and show why population, time, geography, and outcome definition matter. **Gala et al. (2025 version)** make target-variable definition itself a fairness concern. **Mitchell et al.**, **Weinberg**, and **Sambasivan et al.** supply further reasons to avoid equating statistical parity with a complete normative judgment. These should inform the interpretation of Adult and LSAC results: estimation relative to recorded labels does not establish that those labels adequately measure merit, opportunity, or welfare. [Retiring Adult](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.04884), [FairTargetSim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06031), [Mitchell et al.](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-statistics-042720-125902), [Weinberg](https://doi.org/10.1613/jair.1.13196), [Sambasivan et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/3442188.3445896).

### Qualify the statistical contribution by estimand

For the smooth probabilistic contrasts, double robustness of the point estimate follows from the product remainder. It does **not** mean the stated Wald interval remains valid whenever either nuisance is consistently estimated at an arbitrary rate. The manuscript's stronger conditions for asymptotic normality and influence-function consistency belong alongside its coverage claim. Existing targeted-learning references—van der Laan–Rose, Hines et al., and Kennedy—are methodological foundations, not fairness-specific novelty evidence.

For thresholded metrics, your appendix requires a conditional margin bound and sufficiently fast uniform regression estimation. If the regression error is $O_P(r_m)$ and the relevant stratum has boundary mass bounded by $C\epsilon^\kappa$, the stated sufficient condition is $r_m^\kappa=o(n_{\mathrm{eval}}^{-1/2})$. Absence of an atom at the threshold is insufficient. These results should not be advertised as the same generally regular influence-function inference supplied for the probabilistic metrics. Liu–Molinari's discussion of support-function versus support-set estimation is helpful precedent for explaining why estimating decisions is harder than estimating some smooth risk functionals.

At conditional independence, your CMI influence function degenerates to zero. Ordinary first-order Wald inference cannot justify the independence test there. Negative finite-sample estimates are possible for your held-out log-ratio estimator even though population CMI is nonnegative; this is an estimation issue, not negative dependence. The existing simulations explicitly show poor coverage of zero. CMI therefore currently supports a conditional-dependence diagnostic and a discussion of nonregularity, not a reliable certificate of independence or an automatically calibrated feature-selection procedure.

Feature-set comparisons also change the estimand: (D_{P,X_1}) and (D_{P,X_2}) are different conditional regressions. Searching over conditioning sets introduces selection and multiple-testing issues beyond intervals for a prespecified set. FairTest and Cherian–Candès make this limitation particularly visible. Whether a feature should be used is a separate normative or causal question from whether it reduces a conditional-association measure.

## Proposed related-work prose

The following text is suitable for adaptation into an expanded introduction or a separate related-work section. Links identify the references to insert into the bibliography.

> Fairness research distinguishes properties of prediction rules, their underlying data, and the consequences of decisions. Tang, Zhang, and Zhang explicitly separate fairness with respect to the data-generating process, predicted outcomes, and induced impacts. Earlier data-level approaches also address discriminatory training distributions: Kamiran and Calders modify labels or sampling weights, and Calmon et al. optimize transformations under discrimination and distortion constraints. Our goal is to estimate a fairness property of the observed population law for a specified feature set. In particular, the rule under study is the group-blind population regression $D_P(x)=P(Y=1\mid X=x)$, rather than an arbitrary fitted rule or a transformed training distribution. [Tang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.04101), [Kamiran–Calders](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10115-011-0463-8), [Calmon et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03354).
>
> Statistical fairness auditing addresses a complementary question. Cherian and Candès construct simultaneous bounds and certificates for disparities of an independently trained rule across collections of groups. Gao and Gronsbell develop robust semi-supervised inference for fixed-rule fairness metrics using labeled and unlabeled audit data. FairTest searches for unwarranted associations and validates discoveries with attention to multiple testing and explanatory factors. Such procedures can correctly quantify uncertainty about a trained rule without targeting the fairness of the population regression. Our estimating equations additionally correct for estimation of the regression that defines the target. Consequently, a fixed-rule audit and a population-regression analysis can reach different conclusions without either conclusion contradicting the other. [Cherian–Candès](https://jmlr.org/papers/v25/23-0739.html), [Gao–Gronsbell](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12181), [FairTest](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.02377).
>
> Population fairness targets and debiased machine-learning inference are also studied by Liu and Molinari, who estimate the feasible allocation of group risks and the fairness–accuracy frontier over a class of decision rules. Related work characterizes optimal classifiers under demographic-parity constraints and analyzes their estimation. Our targets are instead probabilistic parity and opportunity disparities of the unconstrained population regression. We derive influence-function corrections and nuisance-error remainders for these scalar functionals, and distinguish their regular inference from the additional stability conditions needed when regression scores are thresholded into decisions. [Liu–Molinari](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08879), [Denis et al.](https://jmlr.org/papers/v25/23-0322.html), [Xian et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.01528).
>
> Our analysis is associative and does not identify discriminatory causal mechanisms. Causal fairness approaches instead use potential outcomes, interventions, or pathway restrictions; Rehill applies double machine learning to counterfactually fair prediction, while Ling et al. use causal reasoning for feature selection. We use CMI to examine how conditional dependence varies with the chosen features, building on established dependence measures and mixed-data estimation methods. This does not establish the legitimacy of those features, and first-order Wald inference for CMI is nonregular at conditional independence. Finally, observed-law inference remains dependent on the population sampled and the outcome recorded. Selective-label analyses and work on target definition show why a statistical fairness conclusion need not establish fairness in a different population or with respect to a different substantive outcome. [Khademi et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/3308558.3313559), [Rehill](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11529), [Ling et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/3643890), [Mesner–Shalizi](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.03387), [Kallus–Zhou](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02887), [Gala et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06031).
